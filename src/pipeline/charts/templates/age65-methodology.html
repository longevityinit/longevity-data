This chart shows the average number of years remaining at age 65 for women and men. It uses the death rates recorded in each year, rather than predicting how long a particular group of people will live. Projections are not included.

Best practice is the highest remaining life expectancy among countries and territories with a total population of more than one million that year. The line starts in 1850. Each year needs at least two eligible locations with data for the selected sex. If several locations tie, all are credited. The population cutoff applies to the whole population, not just people aged 65 or older. Countries without population data can still be shown on their own, but cannot contribute to Best practice.

World and regional life expectancy come directly from OWID. We do not calculate them by averaging country values. These series start in 1950.

Percentiles start in 1950. We sort countries and territories from lowest to highest remaining life expectancy, then add up their populations aged 65 or older until we reach the chosen share: 10%, 25%, 75%, or 90%. Women’s lines use women aged 65 or older; men’s lines use men of those ages. The population counts combine the five-year age groups from 65–69 to 100+.

Each percentile takes the life expectancy of the country where that share is reached, without interpolating between countries. Countries of every size are included; regional totals and countries with missing data are left out. The shares are calculated from the population with data available that year. These lines compare country-level life expectancy, not individual lifespans. They are not averages for the bottom 10% or 25% of people. A large country can cause a jump in a percentile line.

Coverage: the earliest observations are for Sweden in 1751. Coverage is limited before 1950, and missing years are left as gaps. This chart uses the same set of countries and territories as the life expectancy at birth chart.

Historical population estimates use present-day borders, which may differ from the territories covered by historical mortality records. We still need to check this match before publishing historical Best practice values.
